Outside Work

I live in Amsterdam with Anna and our golden retriever, Fly. Let me show you around.

Home in Amsterdam

For two years, this was the view from my window. Almost every evening I’d sit there and watch the sun go down behind the windmill. I never got tired of that sky.

I cycle everywhere in Amsterdam. I don’t use public transport here, and I love that even an ordinary ride takes me along the canals and past the narrow houses that made me fall for this city.

You might not believe me, but every time I ride across a bridge over the Amstel, I look down the river and think, “I’m so lucky to be right here, right now.”

Moving here also gave me a habit that’s a little harder to explain.

That explains the missing shirt

The only thing I don’t like about Amsterdam is the weather. To get through the rainy autumns and windy winters, I borrowed an idea from a rather famous Dutchman, Wim Hof, and started taking cold showers. That might explain why I look so happy standing shirtless in the snow. Most of my mountain photos involve a few more layers.

Usually, I look more like this

Helmet, goggles, gloves. This is closer to my idea of what to wear in the snow. I love Amsterdam, but a snowboard is a pretty good reason to leave a city as flat as this one. The view changes quite a bit once that board is under my feet.

Looking the other way

Here’s what I mean. That little bit of board at the bottom is mine, and somewhere down there is the line I get to ride. In summer, I ride the flat routes around Amsterdam on my road bike or my gravel bike. I’m planning to buy a mountain bike, too. For now, I explore the mountain trails on foot.

On foot in the mountains

This is what I love about hiking. I can stop here and take my time looking across the valley. Can you spot both waterfalls? The lower one is half hidden by the trees.

Back on a board

I love boards. Longboards, snowboards, wakeboards, anyboards. The cool thing about wakesurfing is that the boat makes the wave. You can surf on flat water in Amsterdam, in Cyprus, or even in Moscow.

Back in Amsterdam, most weeks it’s basketball on Mondays and Wednesdays, cycling or running. I added swimming to the mix for a triathlon, which is how I ended up with the finish-line photo on the next slide.

Triathlon

About that finish-line photo

On 30 August 2026, I finished IRONMAN 70.3 Zell am See-Kaprun in Austria after a 1.9 km swim, a 90 km ride and a half marathon.

So why am I wearing a bike helmet at the finish line if the last leg was a run? I can be a bit absent-minded. I forgot to take it off after the ride and ran all 21 km in it.

Half the fun was coming up with replies for everyone who pointed it out.

“Mate, you’re still wearing your helmet!”
“I know. Safety first!”

Turns out I love watching, too

By now you’ve probably guessed I like sport. What I didn’t know was how much I’d love the Olympics. Anna talked me into going to the Summer Games in Paris in 2024. I came home an Olympic fan.

In 2026, Anna and I went to Italy for the Winter Olympics. I really hope we make it to Los Angeles in 2028.

Meet Anna

She’s the love of my life. We met in 2011, standing in line to move into the student dorms at Saint Petersburg State University. We started dating later and we’ve been inseparable ever since.

Anna loves drums, dancing and me. The record player was her idea, too. Once we had one, we started collecting vinyl together.

You already know she got me into the Olympics. It’s funny how much of who I am today started with something Anna loved first. The next one might surprise you.

A night at the ballet

Anna also introduced me to ballet. Going to see a performance together has become one of my favourite ways to spend an evening.

Back home with the Taylor

I’ve got my Taylor acoustic, a piano and a drum kit at home. I play all three and collect vinyl, too.

When I settle in with a book, it’s usually science fiction. Dune and The Three-Body Problem are two of my favourites. I love Hard to Be a God and everything else the Strugatsky brothers wrote. Isaac Asimov is another favourite, and the list goes on.

How long I get to read in peace depends on one more member of the household.

And this is Fly

Our golden retriever has his own ideas about how I should spend my evenings. He gets me outside in Amsterdam every day. Which brings us back to the canals, only now you know who gets me out the door to enjoy them.

Amsterdam without vices

Daydreaming in Hygge*

*Hygge is a Danish word that describes a cosy, contented mood evoked by comfort and conviviality.

Good morning, Denmark! We are landing today in the beautiful city of Copenhagen.

  • Let’s try to find out the secret of Danish happiness together.
  • Maybe it is in Danish hot dogs, cosy shops, attractive beer bars, or tidiness and cleanliness?
  • Stop! We haven’t left the airport yet.
  • But I already love it.

Copenhagen surprises you with its tidiness on the streets, in the metro, and everywhere you go. The metro, without drivers, as in the UAE, takes you to the city centre in 20 minutes, as the whole city has 600 thousand inhabitants. One switch to an intercity train, 15 minutes, and we are in a small village near Copenhagen. 300 metres from the North Sea and 50 metres from the train station was our hygge place, with a lot of small details like yoga books, photos of a bike in the mountains on the Scandinavian part of the map, small Lego aeroplanes with a pilot, a guitar, and a cloudy bed.

After dreaming like a baby on the soft bed, we woke up refreshed and ready for breakfast.

A 15-minute train ride and several hundred metres through the old orange military barracks, Nyboder, and you get to Atelier September. With a small queue outside the doors, you can already anticipate the fresh bread and whipped butter you will put in your mouth in several minutes. The mashed egg, butter, cheese, and bread make your morning, and the almond pancakes with blueberry jam finish it. We walk around this city that looks like Amsterdam without its vices: beautiful architecture, green parks, and castles, but no garbage, whores, or marijuana smell. Catholic cathedrals and old palaces are everywhere around the city, and we stumbled upon a carriage parade that looked like the royal family out for a walk.

The most memorable adventure happened when we decided to go to the Louisiana Museum. We jumped on the train, which had been delayed for an hour, with an additional hundred people waiting for it, placed ourselves in the vestibule, and the temperature began to climb like an alpinist in the mountains: slowly but steadily. The train stopped in places it shouldn't have and moved more slowly than Anna on a bike. We grasped at every piece of air that entered when the doors opened. After this journey was over, we walked into the museum of all museums in the world, so the ride was worth it. A deceptively small building welcomed us into a huge state of wonder and joy. We started walking around the yard with a view of the sea, beautiful trees, and sculptures.

Coming to the end of the fence, we found a panorama room, which was the last piece of the exhibition. We found “Memory Lost” by photographer Nan Goldin. This work revolves around abuse, friendship, love, deprivation, and loss. Her work splashes out these feelings, as the artist herself has battled opioid drug addiction and almost died.

The mirror room, with lights hanging down around you, blinking in different colours, water on the floor reflecting those lights along with the ceiling and walls, and the whole performance teleports you to Times Square or Tokyo. After that, we entered a room that is difficult to describe in words: The Visitors, a title taken from the 1981 album by ABBA. Nine friends playing together from separate rooms in a grand old house, the historic Rokeby Farm in upstate New York. They are connected via headphones, and the work, filmed on nine cameras, was taken in a single shot. Everyone had one or two musical instruments: guitar, grand piano, cello, drums, banjo, accordion. The Visitors perform a 64-minute song full of love and loathing, forcing you to cry.

We hid in the corner, sat on the floor, and listened to that music, keeping an eye on the cigar-smoking pianist and Kjartansson playing the guitar in a bathtub full of foam. I imagined this experience under the influence of MDMA or acid and can’t resist coming here again one day.

We saw everything in this museum: the guitar musical ballet “No Tomorrow”, performed by eight girls with Taylor guitars, the same as I have at home. You hear the tension of these strings and fall under the influence of this music, with dances and charming voices. A lot of sorrow: a Swedish band playing a three-minute song, “Sorrow”, for six hours nonstop. We found Georgian artist Niko Pirosmani and several rooms devoted to his works, exhibiting delicious Georgian food, simple peasants, and beautiful views of mountains, villages, and cities.

We had dinner in the restaurant “Scandinavian Pain” with scallops, flounder, and some other fish, with bread, butter, and wine, which were so delicious that you were ready to accept punishment for gluttony. After that, we listened to a live interview with Haruki Murakami, and the most memorable question was: “Haruki, you have written a lot of books. How do you force yourself to write so much, and what do you do if you don’t want to write?” Haruki paused for a moment and said: “First of all, if I don’t want to write, I don’t write. Why would you do something you don’t want to do?”

In the backyard, we found a terrifying performance by a Danish actress. She was dressed in ancient animal skins, reading some old manuscripts in Danish with sound effects, and she sounded like the devil himself. Meanwhile, she completely undressed, but what you saw scared the shit out of you rather than causing sexual arousal because of the overall atmosphere. After dawn, we sat outside on the grass and listened to a thrilling band called LOWLY, with two female vocalists with amazing voices.

I’m afraid there is no museum in the world that will beat this Louisiana Museum of Modern Art, created by Danish entrepreneur Knud W. Jensen in 1958. Tusind tak, Knud!

The last day in Denmark started with fog and the absence of the sun. But we decided to stick to the plan and went to the shore of the North Sea to have breakfast there. While we were sitting on the pier and enjoying Danish pastries, several adult Danish couples came onto the piers, took off all their clothes, and went swimming. Without thinking for too long, and not wanting to stand out from the crowd, we followed their lead and swam naked in the cold North Sea in the morning.

After checking out of our Airbnb, we went to an extremely unusual place for Denmark. This place is inside Copenhagen, and when you leave the metro, you notice that something is different. The streets are dirty, and the crowd is bizarre. You start to notice more vices right outside the metro station in Christiania.

After World War II, this land was abandoned. In 1971, a commune of hippies illegally settled here and created a squat. They announced the independence of this land from the Danish government and created a self-regulated commune called Christiania. The idea of this commune was that they could live without government, a kind of piece of anarchy inside the kingdom. Christiania consists of 15 communes. Every commune nominates a delegate who represents the inhabitants of this commune on the community council. They rule the whole territory and govern relations with Denmark. There is no tax on this territory, and all profits and land belong to the commune, where the commune council decides how to spend them. They had their own currency, but nobody used it, and it became rather a toy for collectors. Denmark tried to get rid of the squatters dozens of times but always failed. Once, the commune even mobilised its own army to resist the government of Denmark. Since then, the Danish police have not regulated it, which has created a lot of drug dealing. Christiania has a long history with drugs. In the 70s, it was easy to get heroin here. There have been a lot of raids by the Danish police on this place, gang shootings, and even a grenade explosion, but in the end, this place still exists. There is only one street where you can buy and sell drugs legally, but only soft drugs. “Pusher Street” is the place where there are several makeshift stalls that look as if several adults decided to play a game of “supermarket”. They put up a piece of wood with a hole, like a counter, drew a sign with the name, and listed the whole menu on a piece of paper.

It looks like you are walking around a film studio in Hollywood where they are shooting a low-budget film about Amsterdam, based on the stories of a homeless person who was passing through there three years ago and can’t remember all the details, but the smell he knows for sure.

This place is amazing. People here resisted the government and chased their dream of freedom and equality. This place is European socialism.

Of course, all the punks, rockers, and other dregs of society could be found here as well.

We sent postcards and went back to the airport.

Denmark is the second-happiest country in the world in all happiness rankings. Why? Because it is hard to be unhappy here!
