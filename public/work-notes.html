Work Journal

I keep notes here on conversations with customers, things we try as a team and what I learn along the way.

Management

The kind of leader I want to be

When I became a manager, I called the man who’d been commercial director at Cisco Russia when I joined. I still remember how I felt coming out of his Friday team meetings. It was Friday, I’d just sat through a meeting, and somehow I wanted to get back to work. Even when a decision from headquarters made no sense to me, he could explain why we were doing it in a way that made me want to give it a go. I used to replay recordings of those meetings to figure out how he did it.

I called to ask for advice. He suggested I read Ashley Goodall, a former senior vice president of human resources at Cisco. I often think about his essay A Study That Changed How I Think About Leadership. And forgive the repetition, but it really did change how I think about leadership.

When we talk about what motivates us, we tend to put things like learning a new skill, doing useful work, or feeling proud of something we’ve done ahead of a pay rise or a promotion. Ask us what motivates someone else, though, and suddenly money and recognition move to the top of the list. The less we know the person, the more likely we are to make that assumption.

I can see how easily that turns into a carrot-and-stick approach to management. Hit your number and you’ll get a bonus. Close this deal and you’ll be on stage with the CEO. Both might sound great. How much do I actually know about what makes the work worth doing for the person I’m saying this to?

I’m still learning to recognise what motivates each person on my team. From the outside, it’s hard to see how much someone enjoys figuring out a difficult problem for a customer. That satisfaction can be a much stronger motivator than money or recognition.

I believe people want to do work they’re proud of, and my job is to give them the resources, coaching and motivation they need, clear what’s in their way, and trust them to decide how to do the work.

I’m still friends with all my former managers, and I’ve borrowed something from each of them. Elsewhere in the industry, I’ve also seen people run teams on the assumption that everyone will slack off unless someone keeps them in line. I’ve seen managers shout at someone who was already struggling, or insist on approving decisions the person closest to the customer could have made themselves. One mistake I particularly want to avoid is changing my mind in five minutes about something that will take somebody else two days to redo.

Those examples come to mind when I’m about to ask for another spreadsheet or tell someone they’re behind on a KPI. What decision will I actually make with that spreadsheet? If someone’s behind, do they know what to try next, and is there something I can take off their plate so they have time to try it?

I get decisions wrong fairly often. Sometimes the decision is fine and the way I handle it is what I regret. I’d like the people on my team to feel comfortable telling me either way. When someone does, I try to listen, even when it’s uncomfortable, and change what I do the next time. I have plenty to learn, but I know the kind of leader I want to become.

Management

How a technique becomes your own

What I love most about sales is how many chances you get to practise. If I’m trying to get better at asking follow-up questions, I can try one on my next call. If it comes out a little awkwardly, the cost is usually small. I can listen to what the customer says, carry on with the conversation and have another go on the next call.

That’s how I like to approach sales. I think of it as a set of skills I can work on individually. I find a lot of them in books. Sometimes I’m halfway through a chapter and a conversation I had months ago suddenly makes sense. My first thought is usually, “I do this too, but mostly by accident.”

When my team and I discussed The Like Switch and a couple of podcasts about it, I heard much the same thing in our one-to-ones. Several people recognised something they already did with customers. They just hadn’t had a name for it. So before moving on to the next podcast, I asked everyone to bring back one example. Who were you talking to? What were you trying to do? What did you actually say, and what happened next? I was just as interested in the attempts that felt awkward as the ones that went well.

Take mirroring, one of the ideas Chris Voss talks about. A customer says, “Getting everyone to agree is the hard part.” You pick up on “getting everyone to agree?” and give them a moment to go on. You might already do that when you’re curious. Try it on purpose for the first time and you can suddenly hear yourself doing it. If you want to hear Voss talk about negotiation, we listened to his conversation on The Knowledge Project.

Once you’ve read about a technique, you first start catching yourself using it by accident. Then you notice when someone uses it with you. The first few times you try it on purpose, it can feel a little awkward. But after a few conversations, there’s a moment when it comes out naturally. From then on, it’s in your toolbox. You can reach for it whenever you think it’ll help.

Technology

A workshop needs preparation

By the time we walk into a customer’s office for a technical workshop, I hope we’ve already done most of the useful work. The day itself matters for a different reason. We get to sit down with people we’ve spent hours talking to, meet some of them in person for the first time and spend a day working together.

We try to get as many people involved on the customer’s side as we can. We start with the people we already know and ask who else we should speak to. On our side, we bring in a few specialists who can get into the details with them. The agenda has to answer two questions. What does the customer want to hear from us? And what do we think is worth bringing to their attention? When we’ve taken their questions seriously, we’ve earned the time to put a few ideas of our own on the table.

The preparation starts with technical discovery, one customer team at a time. We ask them to walk us through the infrastructure they look after, how it connects to the rest and where it gives them trouble. I want to hear about those problems even when they have nothing to do with our product. If we can explain something on the call, we explain it. If we can help fix a problem or get a configuration working, we do it together there and then. Nobody needs to wait three weeks for a workshop to get an answer we can give them today.

How many of those sessions? I’d aim for at least five, with five different teams. I’d rather do twenty-five. Each team sees a different part of the same infrastructure, and I want us to understand how the pieces fit together. Then we take a pause, compare notes and decide what deserves the room’s time. That’s when we put the workshop together and go deliver it.

After the visit, it’s easy to come home pleased with how the day went. There’s still a job to do. We follow up on the questions we left open, the work we agreed to do and the opportunities we uncovered together. The difference now is that the specialists on both sides know each other. They’ve already worked through real problems together, they know who to call, and they have something they both want to get done.
