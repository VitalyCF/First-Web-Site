Work Journal

I keep notes here on conversations with customers, things we try as a team and what I learn along the way.

Management

The kind of leader I want to be

When I became a manager, I called the man who’d been commercial director at Cisco Russia when I joined. I still remember how I felt coming out of his Friday team meetings. It was Friday, I’d just sat through a meeting, and somehow I wanted to get back to work. Even when a decision from headquarters made no sense to me, he could explain why we were doing it in a way that made me want to give it a go. I used to replay recordings of those meetings to figure out how he did it.

I called to ask for advice. He suggested I read Ashley Goodall, a former senior vice president of human resources at Cisco. I still think about one of his essays, The Wonderful Art of Finding What Works.

I recognised a habit in myself when I read it. After a customer call, it’s easy to remember the question I wish we’d asked. It takes a little more attention to notice the question that got the customer talking in the first place. If I can point to that moment and explain what I heard, the person who asked it has something they can try again. I know how motivating it feels when someone notices exactly what I did well.

I believe people want to do work they’re proud of, and my job is to give them the resources, coaching and motivation they need, clear what’s in their way, and trust them to decide how to do the work.

I’m still friends with all my former managers, and I’ve borrowed something from each of them. Elsewhere in the industry, I’ve also seen people run teams on the assumption that everyone will slack off unless someone keeps them in line. I’ve seen managers shout at someone who was already struggling, or insist on approving decisions the person closest to the customer could have made themselves. One mistake I particularly want to avoid is changing my mind in five minutes about something that will take somebody else two days to redo.

Those examples come to mind when I’m about to ask for another spreadsheet or tell someone they’re behind on a KPI. What decision will I actually make with that spreadsheet? If someone’s behind, do they know what to try next, and is there something I can take off their plate so they have time to try it?

I get decisions wrong fairly often. Sometimes the decision is fine and the way I handle it is what I regret. I’d like the people on my team to feel comfortable telling me either way. When someone does, I try to listen, even when it’s uncomfortable, and change what I do the next time. I have plenty to learn, but I know the kind of leader I want to become.

Management

How a technique becomes your own

What I love most about sales is how many chances you get to practise. If I’m trying to get better at asking follow-up questions, I can try one on my next call. If it comes out a little awkwardly, the cost is usually small. I can listen to what the customer says, carry on with the conversation and have another go on the next call.

That’s how I like to approach sales. I think of it as a set of skills I can work on individually. I find a lot of them in books. Sometimes I’m halfway through a chapter and a conversation I had months ago suddenly makes sense. My first thought is usually, “I do this too, but mostly by accident.”

When my team and I discussed The Like Switch and a couple of podcasts about it, I heard much the same thing in our one-to-ones. Several people recognised something they already did with customers. They just hadn’t had a name for it. So before moving on to the next podcast, I asked everyone to bring back one example. Who were you talking to? What were you trying to do? What did you actually say, and what happened next? I was just as interested in the attempts that felt awkward as the ones that went well.

Take mirroring, one of the ideas Chris Voss talks about. A customer says, “Getting everyone to agree is the hard part.” You pick up on “getting everyone to agree?” and give them a moment to go on. You might already do that when you’re curious. Try it on purpose for the first time and you can suddenly hear yourself doing it. If you want to hear Voss talk about negotiation, we listened to his conversation on The Knowledge Project.

I think that awkward bit is worth getting through. First you catch yourself doing something by accident. Then you start hearing it when somebody does it with you. After a few deliberate tries, there comes a conversation where you’re curious about what the customer just said, the question comes naturally, and you only realise afterwards that you used the technique. That’s when it starts to feel like yours.

Technology

A workshop needs a decision

Before a technical workshop, I want us to finish a sentence with the customer. “At the end of this session, we should be able to decide whether…” Sometimes even a partial answer reveals that the person who runs the service needs to be there. Sometimes we discover that a short conversation about the current setup would be more useful than a demo.

Take an authentication change. A happy-path login is easy to demonstrate. What happens to a user whose session expires halfway through an important task? Who gets the support ticket? Agreeing on one awkward case makes a test concrete and gives everyone a sensible way to judge the result.
